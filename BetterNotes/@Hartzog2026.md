###

<span style="background-color: rgba(46, 168, 229, 0.44);">Claim/Argument</span>

> <span style="background-color: rgba(46, 168, 229, 0.44);">we are arguing that AI’s current core functionality—that is, if it is used according to its current design and massive scaled deployment—will act like an aggressive cancer on the institutions that support modern democratic life. The more AI is deployed in our existing economic and social systems, the more the institutions will become ossified and delegitimized. Regardless of whether tech companies intend this destruction, the key attributes of AI systems are anathema to the kind of cooperation, transparency, accountability, and evolution that give vital institutions their purpose and sustainability.7 In short, AI systems are a death sentence for civic institutions, and we should treat them as such.</span>
>
> (p. 730)

###

<span style="background-color: rgba(95, 178, 54, 0.44);">Analysis</span>

> <span style="background-color: rgba(95, 178, 54, 0.44);">we explore three pathologies of AI systems that degrade our core institutions. First, AI systems offload human tasks that demand wisdom and skill onto machines, which undermines and degrades institutionally aggregated expertise. AI systems tantalize people with cheap shortcuts, the illusion of reliability, and the promise of easy and unearned wisdom, which leads to misguided cognitive offloading, skill atrophy or foreclosure, and the frustration of back-end labor required to repair AI’s mistakes and “hallucinations.”9 Second, AI systems automate and streamline important choices,10 which shortcircuits institutional decisionmaking. AI systems outsource moral choices to machines that should be made by humans, flatten the hierarchical structure that privileges persons over things, removing critical points of reflection and conflict. This, in turn, stymies the ability of institutions to take intellectual risks in response to changing circumstances and prevents adaptation. Third, AI systems isolate people by replacing opportunities for human connection, interpersonal growth, and the cultivation of shared purpose. This isolation deprives institutions of the necessary solidarity and the space required for good faith debate and adaptability.</span>
>
> (p. 731)

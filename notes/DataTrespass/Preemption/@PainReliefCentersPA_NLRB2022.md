### In-text annotations

 "On February 23, 2022, the Board issued a Decision and Order finding that the Respondent violated Section 8(a)(1) of the Act by, among other things, discharging five employees for initiating a walkout in protest of the office manager's abusive treatment of them. Pain Relief Centers, P.A., 371 NLRB No. 70, slip op. at 2 (2022) (Pain Relief I). 6 This case involves the lawfulness of a state court lawsuit and discovery requests that the Respondent filed against the five discriminatees shortly after the General Counsel issued the complaint in Pain Relief I. The lawsuit alleged defamation and breach of contract claims. 7" [Page 1](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/JTBQYY9C?page=1&annotation=highlight-p1x53y66)




 "The judge" [Page 1](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/JTBQYY9C?page=1&annotation=highlight-p1x516y66)




 "found that the Respondent's filing and maintenance of the lawsuit and its discovery requests violated Section 8(a)(1), with one exception regarding post-discharge postings by the employees on the jobsite Indeed. com. Specifically, the judge found that the Respondent's defamation claims were largely preempted under San Diego Building Trades Council v. Garmon, 359 U.S. 236 (1959), the Respondent's breach of contract claims were baseless and retaliatory under Bill Johnson's Restaurants, Inc. v. NLRB, 461 U.S. 731 (1983), and the Respondent's discovery requests were unlawful under Guess?, Inc., 339 NLRB 432 (2000). 8" [Page 2](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/JTBQYY9C?page=2&annotation=highlight-p2x54y632)




 "For the reasons stated by the judge, we agree that the bulk of the Respondent's defamation claims are preempted and therefore violated Section 8(a)(1). 9 We further agree with the judge that the Respondent's breach of contract claims violated Section 8(a) (1), although we rely on a different rationale than the judge. The judge found that the breach of contract claims violated Section 8(a)(1) based on Bill Johnson's, above. We find, instead, that they are preempted. 10 Under Garmon, when a state purports to regulate conduct that is arguably protected by Section 7 of the Act or an unfair labor practice under Section 8, “due regard for the [NLRA] requires that state jurisdiction must yield.” 359 U.S. at 244. Here, the Board has already determined that the conduct alleged to be a breach of state contract law--the employees' walkout in protest of the Respondent's office manager's abusive behavior--was not only potentially but actually protected by Section 7. See Pain Relief I, 371 NLRB No. 70, slip op. at 2-3 & fn. 16. Therefore, Garmon preemption is clearly established, and the Respondent violated Section 8(a)(1) by filing and pursuing its breach of contract claim. See 359 U.S. at 245. 11" [Page 2](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/JTBQYY9C?page=2&annotation=highlight-p2x53y471)




 "Finally, we agree with the judge, although primarily on a different rationale, that the Respondent's state court discovery requests violated Section 8(a)(1). The Respondent's discovery requests sought all documents that the Charging Parties in Pain Relief I had submitted to the General Counsel or planned to introduce into evidence, the identity of all persons with whom they had discussed their NLRB case, “all facts you alleged support a violation of the [NLRA],” and all persons whom they had contacted about serving as a witness in their NLRB case. To the extent that the discovery requests are founded on claims that are preempted, we find that they are themselves preempted. To the extent they are founded on the still-intact Indeed.com defamation claims, 12 we find that they are unlawful under Guess?, Inc., which set forth a three-part test for assessing whether discovery requests in a separate proceeding violate the Act. 339 NLRB at 434. First, the questioning must be relevant. Second, if the questioning is relevant, it must not have an illegal objective. Third, if the questioning is relevant and does not have an illegal objective, the employer's interest in obtaining th[e] information must outweigh the employees' confidentiality interests under Section 7 of the Act." [Page 2](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/JTBQYY9C?page=2&annotation=highlight-p2x53y275)




 "Like the judge, we will assume that the Respondent's requests are relevant. However, whereas the judge further assumed that the requests did not have an illegal objective, we find that they did have such an aim. 13 The timing of the discovery requests, which were made within a month of the issuance of the complaint in Pain Relief I, demonstrates a motive to retaliate against the Charging Parties for pursuing unfair labor practice charges and to coerce them into withdrawing the charges. This finding in itself renders the requests a violation of Section 8(a)(1) under Guess?, Inc. See id. But even assuming the requests had a lawful objective, we agree with the judge that they violate the Act because the Respondent's interests in obtaining the information did not outweigh the employees' interests in keeping their Section 7 activities confidential." [Page 2](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/JTBQYY9C?page=2&annotation=highlight-p2x54y138)




 "In contrast, the employees' interests in keeping their Section 7 activities confidential is very strong. Those activities involved the specifics of their participation in a Board proceeding--a core right under the Act. 14" [Page 3](zotero://open-pdf/library/items/JTBQYY9C?page=3&annotation=highlight-p3x54y618)
